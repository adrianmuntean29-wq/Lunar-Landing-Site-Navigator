    Best launch dates for the site

    • Route. Each day the rocket can leave only when its parking orbit lines up with where the Moon will be on arrival: two times a day, computed from the Moon's real position and Earth's rotation.
    • Fuel. The Moon's distance changes from about 356,000 to 407,000 km. Arriving near perigee costs less Δv. The table shows the difference in m/s and the extra propellant in tonnes for the whole stack.
    • Landing site. Only launches that land with the Sun up for the whole stay (crewed) or Sun and Earth up (robotic) are listed.
    • Weather. GO if gusts ≤ 15 m/s, rain ≤ 1 mm, no thunderstorms, freezing rain or snow, and above −10 °C. Real forecast for the next 16 days, after that the share of GO days in 2015–2024.
    • Score. 40% weather, 35% Earth (crewed) or Sun (robotic) at the site during the stay, 25% fuel: full points for the lightest stack, zero for one 5% heavier.

        How this works, and what is simplified

        Trajectory
        Patched conics. Launch due east into a 185 km parking orbit (inclination = launch latitude), translunar injection on a conic that reaches the Moon's distance in the chosen time, insertion into a 100 km polar lunar orbit, powered descent. The crewed mission also climbs back, leaves lunar orbit and returns on a mirrored conic. The 3D view is schematic: Earth's equator and the Moon's orbit are drawn in one plane.
        Sun and Earth over the site
        Real dates. Positions of the Moon and Sun from Meeus' algorithms (main lunar terms, optical and physical libration), then the Sun and Earth seen from the site, including the parallax of Earth. Checked against JPL Horizons on 1–2 Dec 2026 at the IM-2 site: Sun within 0.01°, Earth within 0.03° in azimuth and elevation. There is no atmosphere, so there is no refraction.
        Terrain
        Not computed here. Each site has an assumed local horizon: about 0° on open plains, negative on high peaks that see past the curve of the Moon, large inside craters. The South Pole Navigator computes horizons from terrain, and LOLA data can replace these values.
        Mass
        Rocket equation per stage with dry mass as a fraction of propellant. Crewed: capsule + service module (lunar orbit insertion and return), two-stage lander. Robotic: one lander stage. Translunar stage on top. All inputs are editable above.
        Launch site
        Spaceport Nova Scotia (about 45.35° N, 61.02° W, near Canso; approximate) is under development. No heavy-lift rocket is planned there today, so a lunar mission from Canada is hypothetical. Kennedy and Kourou are there for comparison.
        Launch dates and weather
        Launch times: the site's latitude must reach the parking-orbit plane that contains the Moon's position on arrival (Moon right ascension and declination from the same ephemeris, sidereal time from the IAU formula). Δv uses the real Earth–Moon distance on arrival. Weather comes live from Open-Meteo: its 16-day forecast and ERA5 reanalysis for 2015–2024 at the launch site. Offline, dates are ranked without weather. Real launch rules also cover upper-level winds, lightning and recovery-zone sea state; those are not modelled.
        Shared with the landing calendar
        Sun and Earth seen from the sites come from the team's JPL Horizons table (DE441, every 6 h, interpolated to each hour). The ten sites of the calendar (C1, S, MP1, GR1, six best-lit points in NASA site boxes, and the IM-2 target) use the LOLA horizon masks from our engine, at the chosen mast height. A good landing uses the calendar's example limits: sunlight share, Earth contact share, longest night and Sun height at touchdown. Earth counts as visible when any part of its disc clears the terrain; night means the Sun's centre is below the terrain. The trajectory, launch times and the 3D scene use a simplified model (Meeus ephemeris, patched conics) and do not change the site numbers. The landing replay is illustrative: the descent profile is a smooth curve from 1.5 km, shown about 8× faster. The launch replay uses the real Sun height at the launch site and time for the sky; the rocket, its ascent profile (altitude, speed, Max-Q, staging) and the camera are illustrative, not a flight simulation. The two astronauts during a crewed stay are drawn for scale, not placed by any plan.
        Sites
        South pole: the the calendar's ten sites with LOLA terrain horizons (C1 and S from Gläser et al. 2018, MP1 and GR1 from De Rosa et al. 2012, the best-lit point our engine found in six NASA site boxes from PGDA product 78, and the IM-2 target), plus the IM-1 and IM-2 landing points. Elsewhere: past landings for reference. Every site except those ten uses a flat horizon you can edit in the table (assumed, not measured). Sites you add on the map also use a flat horizon.
